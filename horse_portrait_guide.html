💡 Print a side-profile horse photo for reference. Have it at arm's reach the whole session.
2
Tone the canvas
Start with a warm ground — no scary white
›

Cover the whole canvas with a thin, wiped-back layer of burnt sienna thinned with mineral spirits. Use a rag or big flat brush.

Why?Warms shadows automatically and makes mistakes far less visible
How thin?The canvas weave should faintly show through the color
Drying time15–30 min, or use a hairdryer on low before you sketch
Color?Burnt sienna is traditional. Warm grey or raw umber also work
💡 Toning the canvas is the single biggest beginner tip in oil painting. The warm undertone unifies everything painted on top.
3
Sketch the structure
Loose lines in thinned burnt umber — no pencil
›

Use a thin round brush with burnt umber + spirits to sketch basic shapes. Keep it light — you're mapping, not drawing.

Six shapes to place
1. Skull ovalFills most of the canvas, slightly tilted
2. Muzzle boxExtends down-forward from the oval
3. Eye placementAbout halfway down the skull — not too high
4. Ear trianglesTop of skull, pointing up or slightly forward
5. Neck curveFlows down and off the bottom of the canvas
6. Nostril marksLow on the muzzle, slightly flared ovals
💡 Coach your friends: "Horses have surprisingly long faces — the muzzle takes the lower third." Most beginners draw the head too small.
4
Block in the darks
Establish shadow shapes first — the foundation
›

Mix burnt umber + ivory black for your darkest shadow color. Use a flat #4–6 brush to fill in all shadow areas.

Main dark areas
Inside the ear Under the jaw Around the nostril Below the cheekbone Eye socket Neck underside
💡 Don't blend yet — just flat dark shapes. At this stage it should look "wrong." That's completely normal and part of the process!
5
Add midtones
The color that makes it look like a horse
›

Mix burnt sienna + yellow ochre with a tiny bit of white. Paint the mid-value areas between darks and the lights you'll add next.

Mix guide by coat color
BayMore burnt sienna, less ochre
ChestnutHeavy sienna, hint of cadmium red
PalominoMore ochre + generous white
Grey / roanBlack + white + touch of sienna
💡 Stroke in the direction hair grows — down the face, along the jaw. Even beginners get natural-looking texture this way.
6
Blend & add lights
Soft edges + bright highlights bring it to life
›

While paint is still wet, use a clean dry flat brush to gently blend where darks meet midtones. Then place highlights.

Where to place light
ForeheadTitanium white + tiny ochre, placed boldly
CheekboneCatches the most direct light
Muzzle bridgeSoft warm white strip along the top
Eye highlightTiny bright white dot — makes it feel alive
💡 Less blending = more energy and character. Over-blended paintings go muddy. Visible brushstrokes are beautiful — leave them.
7
Paint the background
Simple is best — don't compete with the horse
›

Fill the background with one muted color. Work carefully around the horse's outline, refining edges as you go.

Good beginner background colors
Muted sage green
Warm grey
Muted blue-grey
Deep warm brown
💡 A slightly darker or cooler background makes the horse "pop." Avoid bright colors — they fight the subject.
8
Details & finishing
Mane, eyes, nostrils — the final touches
›

Switch to a small round brush. These small marks have the biggest impact on the final result.

EyeDark iris + white highlight dot. Add a warm rim of color at the edge
NostrilDark oval, hint of warm pink-red inside
ManeLong flowing strokes — lighter color first, then dark lines on top
WhiskersOptional: thin white/cream lines on the muzzle with a liner brush
💡 Step back often! At arm's length you'll spot what needs adjusting better than up close. And then — sign it. You made art.
